Mage Parab

Festivals & Celebrations · Culture

Mage Parab

A festival of remembering and honouring the ancestors.

𞓧𞓕𞓦𞓤Mage Parab

Paush full moon · January–February

A festival of remembering and honouring the ancestors.

The worship at the ading

Mage falls on the full moon (Purnima) of Pous — the month the Mundas themselves call Mage. By then the winter harvest has been gathered in. The householder fasts the day before, performs his ablutions, and prays at the ading, the sacred inner room of the house, to the household deities — the orabongako, the spirits of the ancestors, who are the main objects of worship at this festival.

S. C. Roy, who described the festival in 1912, prints the prayer the householder offers — for health, for cattle, and for safety in the jungle:

“May we all, young and old, keep well in mind and in body. May our cattle thrive. May neither tigers attack us, nor stubs and stumps of trees nor thorns hurt us when we enter the jungles to gather wood for fuel. May we have plenty to eat and drink. May we have enough to entertain our relatives and friends with, and to pay our dues to landlords and to rulers.”

The festival meal

After the worship at the ading, all the members of the family — and the servants with them — sit together for a hearty meal of rice-flour bread, fried rice (chiura) and molasses (gur); in well-to-do families there are curds and even milk.

The servants’ new year

Mage also closes the working year. After the festival, servants (dhangar) are released from their yearly engagement and go visiting their families, who entertain them warmly. About a fortnight later they are engaged afresh for the new year: the master or mistress drops a little oil on the ground, pours a little on the servant’s head, and sets the oil-cup down; the dhangar anoints himself with the oil, and receives a small cash payment with rice, or a pair of bamboo hair-combs. Roy notes that this ceremony was observed even by non-Mundari masters in the Munda country.

One festival, many villages

Mage is kept beyond the Munda villages too — among the Ho people it is the greatest festival of the year, and Bhumij neighbours also celebrate it — but in the Munda villages Roy described, its heart is the home: gratitude to the ancestors for the gathered harvest. Roy himself cautions that ceremonies vary from place to place; the account above follows his description of the Siri Pargana. If your village keeps Mage differently, please share it with sources.

Sources: S. C. Roy, The Mundas and Their Country (1912), pp. 472–474 — read it free in our Books section; District Khunti (Government of Jharkhand), Culture & Heritage; Encyclopaedia Mundarica. Roy notes that customs vary between villages; his account describes the Siri Pargana of the old Ranchi district. Last reviewed: November 2026.
