Festivals & Celebrations · Culture

Phagu

The festival of the ritual hunt, kept at the full moon of Phalgun — the season of Holi.

𞓑𞓞𞓕𞓦𞓟Phagu

February–March

The festival of the ritual hunt, kept at the full moon of Phalgun — the season of Holi.

The burning castor tree

On the evening before the full moon of Falgun, the young men of the village plant a small erendi — a castor-oil tree (Mundari: jara daru) — on the road leading to the village dari (spring). They cover it from top to bottom with straw and set it on fire; and as it blazes, a crowd of young Mundas runs at it with shouts of merriment and cuts it down with their axes.

Bringing home the semar

The next day most of the young men go out to the forest, singing Japi songs and playing their drums and tom-toms. There they cut down a semar (silk-cotton tree; Mundari: edel daru) and carry it back, singing as they come the well-known song that begins “Madkam Jer do sendera-i-seno-jana”. The young men and maidens who stayed behind come out to meet them, and all return together, singing the Japi songs and dancing the Japi dance. At the spot where the castor tree was burnt, they plant a branch of the semar and cover it over with straw.

The Pahan’s offering — and the hunt

Now the Pahan, the village priest, is called. To all the bongas who preside over the woods, the hills, the streams, the fields and the groves — Jaer Buria, Papra Chandi, Buru Bonga, Ikir Bonga, Marang Buru, Desauli, Chawrani, Nage Era, Bindi Era — he makes joint offerings of three pieces of rice-flour bread, one pot of rice-beer and a black hen, and prays for success in hunting. Then the semar is set on fire and cut down with an axe, and pieces of it are thrown away in the four directions of the compass.

The whole party then goes, singing and dancing, to the Pahan’s house, where he gives them plenty of rice-beer. At home, each head of family worships the spirits of his departed ancestors in the ading of his house, with offerings of bread and rice-beer. And the next day, all the young men of the village go out into the jungle on a hunting excursion — the real hunt that the festival’s fires and fellings have rehearsed.

Phagu in a modern account

The ethnographer Toshiki Osada, recording the festival in the Mundari-speaking village of Keora, describes the rite in a form at once similar and distinct: there, a red chicken — not beheaded, as a sacrifice normally would be — is tossed into a straw-topped shelter of the felled trees and burned, a hunt performed in miniature; before the communal hunt the men pray to Singbonga and to the forest hunting spirit, sendera bir bonga, since the village’s own guardian spirits cannot protect them in the forest; and when the hunters come home, the women welcome them by washing their feet. Osada also records a sign of the times: with the forests thinned and game scarce, the hunters now often return empty-handed.

A note on colours

Phagu takes its name from the Holi season — the word comes from phagu, the coloured powder of Holi — and in some places colours are played today. But in the traditional rite described by Roy in 1912, and in modern scholarship, there is no throwing of coloured water: the heart of Phagu is the fire, the felled trees and the hunt.
