Early life in Ulihatu

Birsa Munda was born on 15 November 1875 in Ulihatu, a village on the Chota Nagpur plateau in present-day Jharkhand. His parents were Sugana Munda and Karmi Hatu.

He grew up in a world shaped by the forest and by shared village land. The Mundas called this system Khuntkatti, in which land belonged to the community rather than to one owner. Over the nineteenth century that world was under pressure, as outside landlords, moneylenders and contractors took over land that Munda families had cleared and farmed for generations.

Birsa was exposed to Christian mission education as a boy, and he also absorbed Munda religious tradition and the example of the earlier Sardar movement, a long Munda protest against the loss of land. Accounts of his schooling differ in detail, but they agree that these influences met in him.

A new faith: the Birsait movement

In 1895, at Chalkad, Birsa declared himself a messenger sent to protect his people. He taught worship of one God, Singbonga, in place of the spirit worship around the village, and he preached cleaner living, unity and independence from outside authority. His followers came to be called Birsaits, and many gave him the title Dharti Aba, Father of the Earth.

The authorities saw a movement with growing influence. Birsa was arrested at Chalkad on 24 August 1895 and sentenced to two years of rigorous imprisonment. He was released in early 1898 and began to rebuild his following almost at once.

His message reached beyond religion. It was a claim that water, forest and land belonged to the people who lived by them.

The Ulgulan, or Great Tumult

By 1899 the Birsait movement had become a political struggle. Birsa spoke of Birsa Raj, a Munda self-rule that would end the authority of landlords, moneylenders and the colonial administration. The uprising began on Christmas Eve, 24 December 1899, when followers attacked churches, police stations and the property of landlords and missionaries.

The Ulgulan was fought with bows, arrows and axes against an army with guns. Its demands were concrete: return of tribal land, an end to forced labour and a halt to unjust revenue policies. Gaya Munda served as a leading commander. The movement spread across Khunti, Tamar, Ranchi and the Chakradharpur area.

Dombari Hill, 9 January 1900

The turning point came on 9 January 1900 at Dombari Hill (Dombari Buru, near Sail Rakab) in Khunti. Thousands of Munda families had gathered there. Colonial forces surrounded the hill and fired on the crowd, and traditional weapons could not answer guns and cannon.

The death toll is still disputed. A report in The Statesman gave about 400 killed, while the administration of the time claimed only a handful of deaths. Local memory keeps the place as a hill of the dead. Birsa escaped into the forest, and a reward was placed on his capture.

A note on sources. Accounts differ on some details. Birsa’s capture at Jamkopai forest near Chakradharpur is dated both 3 February and 3 March 1900. Oral tradition says he was betrayed by an informer.

Capture and death

Birsa was captured in the Jamkopai forest while he slept. He was held in Ranchi jail with hundreds of his followers. He died there on 9 June 1900, aged 25. The authorities attributed his death to cholera. Many accounts doubt this, and his death is still described as mysterious.

The British suppressed the uprising soon after. Many rebels were jailed or executed, and the trial of the hundreds arrested drew public attention well beyond Chota Nagpur.

Legacy

The uprising ended, but its effects lasted. The struggle helped push the colonial government to enact the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act of 1908, which restricted the transfer of tribal land to outsiders. The Act remains a landmark for tribal land rights.

Birsa’s memory is now part of public life. Jharkhand was created on 15 November 2000, his birth anniversary. In 2021 the Government of India named 15 November Janjatiya Gaurav Divas, Tribal Pride Day. His birth anniversary is marked at his Samadhi at Kokar in Ranchi, and a museum stands at the Old Central Jail where he died.

For Mundari communities he is more than a historical figure. He stands for dignity, for the right to the land and for the idea that a people can speak for itself. That is also the purpose of keeping the Mundari language, script and stories alive.
