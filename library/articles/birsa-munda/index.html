Birsa Munda (1875–1900) — Dharti Aaba, Father of the Earth

2026-11-03 · 9 min read · People & Personalities · Mundari Samaj Editorial Desk

Full name: Birsa Munda · Born: 15 November 1875, Ulihatu village (then Ranchi district, Bengal Presidency; now Khunti district, Jharkhand) · Father: Sugana Munda · Mother: Karmi Hatu · Died: 9 June 1900, Ranchi jail (aged 24) · Known as: Dharti Aaba, “Father of the Earth” · Movement: the Ulgulan (1899–1900)

Early life and family

Birsa was born into a poor Munda family that moved from village to village in search of work as labourers and crop-sharers — from Ulihatu to Kurumbda and Bamba, where his elder brother Komta and his sisters were born. By the custom of the time he was named after the day of the week on which he was born. As a boy he grazed sheep in the Bohonda forest, learned the flute and the one-stringed tuila, and spent his evenings at the akhara, the village wrestling ground. For a time he lived at Ayubhatu, his maternal uncle's village.

Schooling and the mission years

Birsa began school at Salga, under the teacher Jaipal Nag, who saw his sharpness and recommended him to the German Mission School. To join it, Birsa converted to Christianity and took the name Birsa David, later Daud. After a few years he left the school. The years at Chaibasa (1886–1890) were formative: the Sardar agitation against the erosion of Munda land rights was in the air, and around 1890 Birsa and his family gave up mission membership and returned to their traditional tribal faith.

The land question

Under the traditional khuntkatti system, the descendants of a village's founding families held the land together. Colonial rule — and the landlords, thikadars and moneylenders who came with it — broke that system down: rents, forced dues and forest restrictions (protected forests were declared under the Indian Forest Act in 1893–94) pushed many Mundas from owners of their land to labourers on it. Birsa had seen this dispossession with his own eyes as his family moved in search of work, and it shaped everything he did next.

The Birsait faith

In 1895, at Chalkad in Tamar, Birsa renounced Christianity and began preaching: worship one God, give up the worship of bongas (spirits), and return to the community's own path. Followers — Mundas, Oraons and Kharias — flocked to Chalkad; he gained a reputation as a healer and preacher, and the new faith took his name: Birsait. His people called him Dharti Aaba. His slogan, remembered across the region to this day, was “Abua raj seter jana, maharani raj tundu jana” — “Let our rule begin; let the Queen's rule end.”

Arrest and prison, 1895–1897

The colonial administration moved quickly. Birsa was arrested on 24 August 1895 and sentenced to two years' imprisonment. After his release he spent about two years moving between villages, holding secret meetings and rebuilding the movement, while missionaries and officials pressed for his arrest again.

The Ulgulan — the Great Tumult

Around Christmas 1899, some 7,000 men and women assembled to herald the Ulgulan — the “Great Tumult” — a rising for Munda self-rule and the recovery of their land, forests and water. The rising spread across Khunti, Tamar, Basia and Ranchi. On Christmas Eve 1899, followers attacked missions at Murhu and Sarwada and tried to burn churches in Ranchi and Singhbhum districts. On 5 January 1900 two police constables were killed at Etkedih, and on 7 January the Khunti police station was attacked. The administration set a reward of Rs 500 on Birsa's head.

Dombari Buru, 9 January 1900

Thousands of Birsa's followers gathered at Dombari Buru, a hill in Khunti, armed largely with bows and arrows. Colonial troops under Commissioner A. Fobes and Deputy Commissioner H. C. Streattfield surrounded the hill and opened fire. In community memory it was a massacre: The Statesman reported around 400 killed, and the Mundas named the hill Topped Buru — “the mound of the dead”. Birsa himself escaped into the Singhbhum hills.

Capture and death

Birsa was arrested on 3 February 1900 in the Jamkopai forest in Chakradharpur and brought to Ranchi. Some 460 tribals were accused in 15 cases; 63 were convicted. Birsa died in Ranchi jail on 9 June 1900, aged 24. Colonial records attributed his death to cholera and dysentery; among his followers, suspicion of poisoning has persisted ever since.

Legacy

The movement faded after his death, but its pressure contributed to the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act of 1908, which prohibited the transfer of tribal land to non-tribals — a protection that still matters today. Birsa's portrait hangs in the Indian Parliament Museum, and the old Ranchi jail where he died is now the Birsa Munda Smriti Sangrahalaya (memorial museum), opened in 2021. Jharkhand state was formed on his birthday, 15 November 2000, and since 2021 the day is observed across India as Janjatiya Gaurav Diwas. The standard scholarly study of his life remains K. S. Singh's “Birsa Munda and His Movement”.
