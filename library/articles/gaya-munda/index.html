Among the men closest to Birsa Munda, none is remembered in Khunti with more pride than Gaya Munda of Etkedih. When the police came for him in January 1900, his whole household — wife, sons, daughters and daughters-in-law — fought them with whatever weapons they had. The stand at his house is one of the best-documented episodes of the Ulgulan, yet beyond those few days the written record of his life is thin, and much repeated about him is later retelling. This article keeps to what can be documented — and says where the record ends.

The Sardar of Etkedih

Gaya Munda was a Birsaite sardar and one of Birsa's trusted lieutenants, named in the literature alongside Manjhia Munda and Donka Munda. His home was Etkedih village — spelled Etkdih, Itkadih or Aitkedih in different records — in the Khunti country south of Ranchi. Popular accounts call him the commander of Birsa's forces, active since the Sardari days; those details rest on retelling, not contemporary documents. His standing is not in doubt: when the authorities struck back, he was among their first targets.

5 January 1900: the arrest party

On 5 January 1900 a police party was sent to Etkedih to arrest Gaya Munda. In the dry bed of the Tajna river the party was attacked by Gaya Munda and other Birsa supporters, and two constables were killed. It was the moment the Ulgulan turned from raids on churches into open confrontation with the state.

6 January: the house at Etkedih

The next day, 6 January, the Deputy Commissioner of Ranchi, H. C. Streattfield, came with a large police party and surrounded Gaya Munda's house. Inside were Gaya, his wife Maki, his sons, his daughters Thingi, Nagi and Lembu, his daughters-in-law and grandchildren. Called on to surrender, the family refused, and fought with sticks, swords, axes and bows; the Deputy Commissioner's own report, quoted in later histories, describes the women fighting with sticks and axes and credits Maki Munda with killing a policeman with a thrown axe. The house was set on fire, the family driven out, and Gaya Munda captured. A modern scholarly study, drawing on the judicial records, notes that the defence of the household by its women is 'well documented in the official records' — a striking admission from the very administration that crushed the rising.

Trial, fate and memory

Gaya Munda was tried with the other Birsaite prisoners. Accounts based on the trial records state that he was sentenced to death, and he is remembered in Khunti as having been hanged in Ranchi jail; the women of his family were sentenced to rigorous imprisonment. The official statistics of the trials, which record only one death sentence among 63 convictions, sit uneasily beside these accounts; the discrepancy remains unresolved. What is certain is that Gaya Munda never returned to Etkedih. In community memory he stands as Birsa's commander — the head of a family that, women and men together, chose to fight rather than surrender.
