The Kol Rebellion (1831–1832) — Chotanagpur rises

2026-11-04 · 7 min read · History · Mundari Samaj Editorial Desk

In December 1831, the villages of Chotanagpur rose in one of the earliest great Adivasi uprisings against the colonial order in eastern India — two generations before Birsa Munda's Ulgulan. The British called it the "Kol" rebellion, but "Kol" was their collective label for several distinct peoples: the Munda, Oraon, Ho and Bhumij communities of the plateau, joined in places by Kharwar and Chero neighbours. It was never one tribe's revolt; it was a land rising shared across communities.

The spark in Sonpur

The immediate cause lay in Sonpur pargana. Harinath Shahi, brother of the Nagvanshi king of Chotanagpur, Jagannath Shah Deo, had granted away village lands in Sonpur to outsiders — Sikh horse traders and a Muslim cloth merchant — to raise money. Among the villages handed over were twelve belonging to Singrai Manki and Mohan Manki, hereditary Manki holders who were dispossessed at a stroke. The new revenue farmers squeezed the raiyats with illegal extra cesses; Captain Thomas Wilkinson, who later suppressed the rising, himself recorded that landlords and contractors had pushed land revenue up by about a third. For families whose claim to the soil went back to the first clearers of the forest, this was not a rent dispute — it was dispossession.

The rising

In December 1831 the farms of two Sikh thikadars in Sonpur were plundered and burnt, and the rising spread rapidly across Ranchi, Hazaribagh, Palamau and Manbhum and into Singhbhum. Its leaders included Budhu Bhagat, an Oraon religious leader from Silagai village who fought a guerrilla campaign, and Mankis such as Joa Bhagat, Jhindrai Manki and Bindrai Manki. Budhu Bhagat was killed in February 1832 at Silagai, and under Wilkinson's command the main rising was broken by the spring of 1832 — though in the Ho country of Kolhan, resistance flickered on for years, until Poto Ho's rising of 1836–37 was also put down.

A new way of ruling

The British understood that Chotanagpur could not be governed like the plains. By Regulation XIII of 1833 they created the South-West Frontier Agency — a "non-regulation" territory outside the ordinary Bengal laws, working from 1834 with its headquarters at Kishunpur in Ranchi and Captain Wilkinson as its first Agent. In 1837 the Kolhan Government Estate was formed in the Ho country: 622 villages with headquarters at Chaibasa, administered through the traditional Manki–Munda system under a set of instructions known as Wilkinson's Rules — an official admission that the old village and pir leadership had to be worked with, not simply replaced. In 1854 the Agency became the Chota Nagpur Division, the framework within which the region was governed for the rest of the century.

Why it matters

The questions of 1831 — who owns the land, whose authority counts, and what happens when outsiders (dikus) take both — did not go away. They returned in the Sardari Larai of Birsa Munda's father's generation and in the Ulgulan itself. And the rising carries a lesson in naming: "Kol" hides the real communities inside a colonial shorthand. On this website we name them — Munda, Oraon, Ho, Bhumij — as they named themselves.
