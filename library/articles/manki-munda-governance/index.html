Manki–Munda: Traditional Governance

2026-11-04 · 4 min read · Heritage · Mundari Samaj Editorial Desk

Long before district offices reached Chotanagpur and Kolhan, Munda and Ho villages governed themselves. Authority was layered and local: each village had its headman, groups of villages stood under a wider chief, and councils of elders settled disputes by custom rather than written law. Beside this secular order stood a second, sacred one, led by the village priest. Together — Munda, Manki and Pahan — they formed a system of self-governance that in Kolhan survives, in adapted form, to this day.

The village: the Munda and the council of elders

S. C. Roy's The Mundas and Their Country (1912) describes each Munda village as occupied by the descendants of the family that had first cleared its land. Its headman, the Munda, was the village's secular head. He did not judge alone: a village Panch, a council of elders, arbitrated disputes with him. Custom was the recognised law; offences drew fines and, in extreme cases, expulsion from the village community. In the Ho country of Kolhan the same office exists today: one hereditary Munda per village, resolving its disputes.

The parha: the Manki, first among equals

Above the village stood the parha, or patti — a group of villages acting together. Roy describes villages grouped 'generally of twelve — but sometimes more and sometimes less', with the strongest of their headmen becoming the Manki, or patti-chief, to whom the others owed allegiance. In Kolhan the corresponding unit is the pir, generally eight to fifteen villages; a settlement record quoted in the Mora Ho judgment counted 26 pirs in the Kolhan estate. The Manki presided over the Patti-Panch or Parha-Panch, the council for disputes between villages and matters of tribal importance. Yet Roy is careful: the Manki, like the Munda, 'was always looked upon as a chief among equals — a leader and not a ruler', and no superior rights of property belonged to his office. Both offices, first filled by choice of the strongest, gradually became hereditary.

The Pahan: the village's other pillar

Village leadership had two halves. Beside the Munda, the secular head, stood the Pahan — in Roy's words, the ecclesiastical head, the village priest. The Pahan led the village's worship: the offerings to the village deities and the spirits of the place. Roy suggests that in the earliest remembered order the two functions were not yet separated, and the founder's heir was chief in temporal as well as spiritual matters. The division that followed — Munda for order, Pahan for the sacred — is still recognised in Munda villages.

1833–1837: Wilkinson's Rules and the Kolhan settlement

After the Ho and Kol risings of the early nineteenth century, the British, unable to subdue Kolhan by force alone, chose to work through its traditional leaders. Captain Thomas Wilkinson, the Company's Political Agent, drafted a set of 31 rules in 1833 as guiding principles for the Ho leaders — described as the first formal codification of any adivasi community's traditional system. When the Kolhan Government Estate was created in 1837, the rules took effect there, and a Hukuknama — a record of rights — recorded the duties of Mankis and Mundas. Recognition came at a price: leaders who had never collected revenue became intermediaries of the colonial administration, and ideas of individual property entered with them.

Custom, not statute: the Mora Ho judgment

Remarkably, Wilkinson's Rules were never formally sanctioned. In Mora Ho v. State of Bihar (2000), a Full Bench of the Patna High Court held by majority that the Rules had not been framed by the Governor-General in Council — the only authority competent under Regulation XIII of 1833, and therefore lacked statutory force. The settlement reports of Ranchi (1910) and Kolhan (1913–1918) had themselves recorded that the Rules were framed 'though not sanctioned by the Government'. Yet the Court noted the Rules had been followed in Kolhan's civil justice for about 150 years, and directed that they continue until suitable replacements were framed. In Kolhan, then, the Manki–Munda order rests on custom — on a century and a half of practice — rather than on any statute.

The system today

The Kolhan Government Estate was dissolved after Independence, but Wilkinson's Rules continued in Kolhan's civil justice, the region remaining largely exempt from general civil procedure laws. In 2021 the Jharkhand government recognised the traditional Nyaya Panch for revenue-related work such as reporting land transactions and settling disputes. That balance is still negotiated: in September 2025, Ho villagers in West Singhbhum protested over feared interference with the system; the administration responded that Mankis and Mundas remain integral to it. Within the community there is also discussion about how a hereditary system should adapt to modern administration. The system endures not as a museum piece, but as a living institution whose terms are still debated by the people it governs.
