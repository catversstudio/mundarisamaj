As recorded by S. C. Roy in The Mundas and Their Country (1912), the Munda tribe was divided into “a large number of exogamous groups called kilis”. A killi (Roy also spells it kili, and calls it a sept or clan) was a brotherhood: by tradition, all its members were descended from one common ancestor, and descent was traced through the father. This article draws on Roy's 1912 ethnography and the 1961 Census monograph of Jojohatu village. Both are historical records; clan practice today varies from family to family and place to place.

One ancestor, one brotherhood

Roy describes the members of a killi as “bound to stand by one another in weal or woe”. They shared property in common, joined in common worship, and buried their dead in a common sasan (burial ground). An injury done to any one member from outside “came to be looked upon as a common injury”, and would be resented and avenged by all. Roy adds that many stories were still told of how a killi combined, in earlier times, to punish wrongs done by outsiders to its members.

How a killi gets its name

According to Roy, distinctive killi names were coined “from some memorable incident connected with the clan”, or from some circumstance of religion or belief, and many names carry an origin story. The ancestors of the Tuti killi, crossing a swollen river in winter, sent burning charcoal across to their shivering kinsmen in a forked twig of the tuti plant; the men, relieved from the cold, vowed never to eat that plant again and formed a separate killi. The Horo or Kachua (tortoise) killi took its name from a tortoise that carried the clan's ancestor, his family and his luggage across a flooded river: “No Munda of the kili will kill a tortoise or eat its flesh.” The Nag killi honoured a white Nag serpent that, in the story Roy records, carried a dead snake-charmer's body home to his sons; no member of that killi would injure a Nag serpent. Other names Roy mentions include Bando, the wild cat, and Tan or Tau, the sparrow.

Marriage must go outside the killi

The killi was exogamous: as Roy puts it, marriages between descendants of the same common paternal ancestor were interdicted, so a spouse always had to be found in another killi. As the clans grew enormous, Roy suggests, they subdivided into separate kilis, in part so that members would not have to travel far into another clan's country “to seek wives for their sons or husbands for their daughters”. Within the wider community the boundary ran the other way: by the custom Roy records, Mundas did not marry into the other ‘Kolarian’ tribes — Santals, Kharias, Asurs or Bhumijes — with a local exception for the Bhumijes of Pargana Pitkum, adjoining Tamar, who intermarried with the Mundas of that side. The Jojohatu monograph shows the exogamy rule had teeth: it recorded a boy and a girl of the same gotra who fell in love; when the girl conceived, the villagers unanimously decided the couple should be evicted, and they left the village to the beating of drums.

The clan in village life

The killi was also a working institution. When members of one killi founded new villages, Roy records, they at first went on burying their dead in the common sasan of the parent village and joined in public worship at the parent village's Sarna; allied villages of the same killi formed a parha, which became, in his words, “the unit of social as well as political organisation”, acting as one body in social and administrative matters. The parha's Panchayat judged breaches of the marriage laws, disputes over burial rights in the sasan, inheritance and boundary disputes. Yet Roy notes that social matters could not be decided by ‘kili-hagas’, clan brethren, alone: the opinion of ‘kutumbs’ — men of other kilis, with whom alone marriage relations could be entered into — had also to be consulted. The 1961 Census monograph of Jojohatu village gives the same structure at village scale: its traditional Panchayat, it says, consisted of members of the killi and used to be convoked by the Manki and the Pahan. Jojohatu's own Mundas were mostly of the Hassa killi — though a village story said they had originally been Hansda, changing to Hassa at the time of the first Revenue Settlement to distinguish themselves from their Hansda neighbours.

Roy's account dates from 1912 and the Jojohatu monograph from 1961. They are preserved here as records of what those observers found; how far any family or village follows these clan rules today is something each community can best say for itself.
