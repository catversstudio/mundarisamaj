As recorded by S. C. Roy in The Mundas and Their Country (1912), “All the distinctively Mundari religious festivals, with the single exception of the Kadleta, have each its appropriate dance and songs. ” Half a century later, the 1961 Census monograph of Jojohatu village still described participation in community singing and dancing as “the most sought-for form of entertainment during leisure hours”.

A round of dances through the year

Roy names the principal classes of Mundari songs and dances as the Mage or Jarga, the Jadur, the Japi, and the Lahsua or Karam — set in a yearly round turning with the festivals. The Mage dances and songs begin after the Sohrai festival in Kartik (October–November) and continue up to the Mage festival in December, the last fortnight being specially called Jarga. From then, the Jadur and Gena songs and dances are taken up until the Sarhul or Ba-parob festival in Chait (March–April) — one Gena being sung after every two Jadur songs. After Sarhul come the Japi or hunting songs and dances, for two or three weeks, the season of the youths' hunting excursions. Then follow the Lahsua or Karam dances and songs, which go on right up to Sohrai again. “Thus,” Roy writes, “the Mundas have a regular round of still-returning dances all the year through.”

Standing dances and stooping dances

The Mundas classified their dances by posture, Roy explains, into the Tingu Susun-ko, the standing dances, and the Ungud Susun-ko, the stooping dances. The Jadur is a running dance (Nir-Susun): the dancers stand upright and run in a circle from right to left. The Gena, moving in a circle, and the Japi, moving in a straight line, go at a more moderate speed and are standing dances proper. The Lahsua or Karam dances are stooping dances: the dancers join hands, stoop forward and form an arc of a circle, advancing and retiring in that posture. Its subdivisions are the Khemta, “very slow and graceful”, and the Bin-sari, danced from cock-crow to sunrise, more erect. Some dances, Roy notes, pantomime agricultural operations such as reaping; and it is not religious exultation alone that prompts them — “social joy and merriment also finds rhythmic expression in the steps of the dance”. There are special dances for marriage festivals too (arandi-susun), one of them named the Dom-kachi.

The akhra: the village's open-air hall

The stage for all this was the akhra. Roy describes the akhra as an open space, usually near the middle of the village, under an old wide-spreading tree — where public meetings and Panchayat sittings were held, and where “the young folk of the village assemble in moon-lit nights and on festive occasions to dance and sing”. The 1961 Census monograph of Jojohatu village found the same institution in place: the akhra an open space about thirty feet square, with raised stone slabs on one side as benches — “the place for dance-meets in which both men and women participate” and “also the venue of social gatherings in the village”. It catches the akhra's evening sound too: no sooner have the people settled after their night meals than “beats of the drum may be heard from the Akhara, and gradually people trickle to the place in groups of twos or threes”.

The instruments the records name

Roy lists the instruments of his day. The drums are the dholki, small, of wood and goat-skin; the nagera, a large iron drum with ox or buffalo hide; and the dumang, earthen-framed, with monkey-skin heads. With them go the dhanpla (tambourine) and the kartal (brass cymbals); the saranga fiddle; the tuhila, a one-stringed banjo of pumpkin gourd, and the banam, a two-stringed banjo of two gourds; and the bamboo flutes, the rutu and the smaller murli. At paiki dances, young men wore ankle-bells called ghugura. At marriage festivals, Roy says, the Mundas employed musicians of the Ghasi community, whose principal instruments were the dhink drum, the narsingha horn and the perened pipe. The Jojohatu monograph, for its part, mentions only “beats of the drum” at the akhra, without naming the instrument.

Songs on the page, and change in the air

Roy's book also prints Mundari song texts with English renderings, including Jadur love-songs. The Jojohatu monograph's Karma festival likewise ends in dance — all-night singing and dancing, and next day the dancers going from house to house, received at each with a cup of handia. Yet the monograph also caught change in 1961: Jojohatu's schoolboys, it records, were not allowed to join the dancing — though the young Munda headmaster himself was an active participant.

Both books are historical records — Roy's of 1912, the monograph's of one village in 1961 — and how each village keeps its akhra and its festival round today is for that village to describe.
