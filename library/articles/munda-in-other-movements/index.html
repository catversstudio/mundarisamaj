The Ulgulan of 1899–1900 was the most famous Munda rising, but it was neither the first nor the last time Munda men and women joined a wider struggle. Again and again — in the Sardari Larai of the nineteenth century, in the Tana Bhagat movement after 1914, and in the twentieth-century movement for a Jharkhand state — Mundas acted alongside their Oraon, Ho, Santhal and Kharia neighbours. This article traces those shared movements, keeping one distinction clear throughout: who led a movement, and who joined it.

The Sardari Larai (1858–1895): the struggle of petitions

The Sardari Larai — the 'sardars' struggle' — was a forty-year agitation by Munda and Oraon chiefs and tenants against the landlords, moneylenders and contractors who were taking tribal land. Its leaders argued that the Adivasis, as the first clearers of the Chotanagpur forests, were the rightful owners of the soil. The historian Suresh Singh describes the movement in three phases: an agrarian phase of clashes over rising rents, evictions and harassment; a revivalist phase; and a political phase in which, from 1890, the sardars turned against European officials and missionaries too, believing them hand in glove with the landlords.

For most of its life the Sardari Larai was fought with petitions and lawsuits rather than weapons: between 1858 and 1895 the sardars appealed to the highest authorities in the land, including the Viceroy and the Secretary of State for India in London. It won little. When a conspiracy of September 1892 to attack contractors and missionaries collapsed for want of organisation, the sardars' following began to look for a leader who would go further — and found him in Birsa Munda, whose own father is believed to have been a Sardari sympathiser. The Ulgulan grew directly out of the Sardari Larai's unfinished business.

The Tana Bhagat movement (from 1914): an Oraon renewal that Mundas joined

In April 1914, in Chingri village in the Gumla country, a 25-year-old Oraon named Jatra Bhagat proclaimed that Dharmes — God — had appeared to him in a vision. The Oraons were to worship God alone through prayer and devotion, give up the worship of spirits, abandon animal sacrifice and liquor, and prepare for the coming Oraon Raj, of which he would be the first king. His followers, the Tana Bhagats, refused to work for the zamindars or the government. Jatra and his leading disciples were arrested, tried and imprisoned, but others carried the movement on, and it spread across the Oraon country of Chotanagpur.

This was an Oraon movement, led by Oraons — but it did not stop at the boundary of one community. Munda and Kharia families living alongside the Oraons also joined the Tana Bhagats, drawn by the same grievances over rent and forced labour, and by the promise of a purified, self-respecting faith; the anthropologist S. C. Roy recognised it as a major new religious movement as early as 1921. In the 1920s the Tana Bhagats found a wider cause: many threw in their lot with Gandhi's movement, taking part in Satyagraha, the Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience campaigns, and carrying their own no-rent message into the national struggle.

The road to Jharkhand

The twentieth century turned the old language of land and dignity into organised politics. In 1938 the region's scattered Adivasi organisations came together on a single platform — the body that, as the Adivasi Mahasabha, became the political centre of the Jharkhand movement. Its leading figure was a Munda: Jaipal Singh Munda of the Khunti country, Oxford-educated, who had walked away from the Indian Civil Service and captained India's hockey team to gold at the 1928 Amsterdam Olympics. Invited to preside over the Mahasabha in 1939 and honoured as Marang Gomke — 'Great Leader' — he demanded a separate province of Jharkhand, carved out of Bihar, where Adivasi land, language and custom would be protected.

The Mahasabha united leaders of the Ho, Munda, Santhal and Oraon communities, though its most famous voice was a Munda's. After independence it was reorganised as the Jharkhand Party (1949–50), and Jaipal Singh carried the Adivasi case into the Constituent Assembly itself. He died in 1970, thirty years before his goal was reached; the movement passed through many hands after him, but on 15 November 2000 — Birsa Munda's birth anniversary — Jharkhand at last became a state. A movement the sardars had begun with petitions in 1858 had, a century and a half later, redrawn the map of India.
