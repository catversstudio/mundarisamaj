What follows is not a description of a Munda wedding of today. It is a summary of the marriage ceremonies described by S. C. Roy in The Mundas and Their Country (1912), together with the briefer customs he recorded for divorce, remarriage and birth. Roy himself warned of local variations between parganas, and much has changed since 1912.

First, the omens: Chenre-uri

In Roy's account, matches were ordinarily arranged by the father or guardian, though in earlier times, he notes, young people had a freer choice among the marriageable kilis. When a father had a suitable bride in view for his son, he sent a go-between called a Dutam to the girl's guardian. A day was then fixed for the Chenre-uri, the omen-reading. The boy's party walked to the girl's village marking every omen on the way. Among good omens Roy lists a cow and her calf lowing in answer to each other, and pitchers of water being carried; among bad ones, a man carrying an axe or spade, and a tree falling under the woodcutter's axe. One bad omen was enough: the party turned back at once and the negotiations fell through. If the omens held, the guests were seated on mats in the courtyard, their feet washed by the women of the house, and feasted with rice-beer, boiled rice and pulse. On a return visit, a spokesman — the joarni — declared the match in a set speech before Sing Bonga and the Panch: “the boy's father and the girl's father will thatch two roofs with one bundle of straw” — the two families made one.

The betrothal (Bala) and the bride-price, gonong-taka

At the Bala or betrothal, Roy records, the bride-price was settled not by naming figures but by symbols — clay marbles for the rupees wanted, rolled sal leaves tied with coloured thread for the saris. The groom's guardian kept some tokens and returned the rest, showing how much he agreed to give. The two guardians then embraced, and the Munda or Pahan of the bride's village clasped hands with his counterpart of the bridegroom's village, each binding himself to a money fine if he broke the betrothal. The bridegroom-elect was seated on the knees of his betrothed's maternal uncle and received a new cloth and a bead necklace. Later, the bride-price, the gonong-taka, was carried to the bride's house (the Gonongtaka-Idituka), and the Logon-tol fixed the wedding day.

The wedding day: Sarandi

A few days before the wedding, a mud pulpit called a mandoa was raised in the courtyard of both houses, with sal saplings at its corners and centre. Bride and bridegroom were anointed with turmeric at their own homes (the Sasang-goso), and a drop of blood from each one's little finger dyed two small rags called sinai. At the bride's house came the Sinduri-Rakab: the couple were carried three times round the mandoa and made to stand on sal-leaf plates, she facing east and he west; he pressed the toes of her right foot with his left foot and touched her neck three times with his blood-dyed rag, and she did the same to him. They exchanged garlands of gulaichi flowers, each put three vermilion marks on the other's forehead, and their garments were knotted together. Then four unmarried girls fetched water from a tank or river, escorted by women with sword and bow and by Ghasi musicians; in the Dul-da ceremony the couple, seated on yokes beside a plantain tree, anointed each other with oil and turmeric and were bathed with that water.

Divorce, remarriage — and a note on birth

Roy also records how a marriage could be dissolved. In the Sakam-Chari divorce, a Panchayat was convened and the party unwilling to continue the marriage tore a sal leaf in two; in Pargana Tamar a piece of turmeric was broken as well (Sasang-haid). If the wife was the unwilling party, the bride-price (gonong-taka) and the tora-taka had to be returned. A widow, Roy says, could remarry only in the simpler Sagai form, without the Sarandi ceremonies: the bridegroom came with some relatives to the bride's house, the party was feasted, and he took the bride home; in some localities the couple simply put sindur marks on each other's foreheads. Roy also records birth customs: a fowl was set apart in pregnancy and rice-beer brewed for Garasi Bonga, guardian of expectant mothers; on the eighth day came the Chati purification; and at the Sakhi name-giving, grains of rice were dropped into water in the names of ancestors — the baby was named after the ancestor whose grain met the baby's grain, and that relative became the child's sakhi or mita (namesake).

This is a 1912 record of Chotanagpur practice, offered as history; today's weddings blend old and new as each family chooses.
