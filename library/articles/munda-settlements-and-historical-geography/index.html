Introduction

The Munda people live across a broad region of eastern and central India. Their present-day settlement geography reflects long histories of village formation, movement, interaction with neighbouring communities, changes in land use, labour migration and modern administrative boundaries.

A map of present-day Munda settlements is not the same as a map of ancient Munda territory. Historical geography must distinguish between current population distribution, older settlement accounts and hypotheses about much earlier population movements.

1. The wider regional setting

Munda-speaking communities are especially associated with Odisha and Jharkhand. They are also found in West Bengal, Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh and other regions. Migration for work, education, industry and other reasons has contributed to settlement beyond older concentration areas.

Linguistic scholarship notes that Munda languages are distributed across parts of central and eastern India. It also cautions that the prehistory and earlier geographical extent of Munda-speaking populations remain uncertain.

2. Munda communities in Odisha

Odisha is an important region for Munda history and language documentation.

The Odisha State SCST Research and Training Institute identifies Sundargarh, Sambalpur and Keonjhar among the major locations of Munda communities. The Odisha State Tribal Museum's Munda profile lists Sundargarh, Sambalpur, Keonjhar and adjoining districts. Its separate Mundari profile lists Sundargarh, Mayurbhanj and parts of Keonjhar.

These sources use different administrative and community categories. Their population figures and labels should not be combined without checking what each one counts.

3. Mayurbhanj and the need for local research

Mayurbhanj deserves a dedicated place in the Mundari Samaj archive. Institutional sources identify Mundari communities in Mayurbhanj, while Odisha's wider tribal geography shows the district as an important area of Adivasi settlement.

A careful Mayurbhanj research project should document:

  • village names and local spellings;
  • community self-identification;
  • languages spoken by different generations;
  • local origin and settlement traditions;
  • old land and revenue records, where accessible;
  • schools, community institutions and cultural organisations;
  • migration histories and links with neighbouring districts;
  • local festivals, songs and place-based traditions.

No single general profile can replace village-level research. The archive should not claim that all Munda families in Mayurbhanj share one migration route or one settlement date.

4. Districts and settlement evidence

Government and institutional profiles identify Munda or Mundari communities in several Odisha districts. The Odisha State Tribal Museum lists Munda in Sundargarh, Sambalpur, Keonjhar and adjoining districts. Its Mundari profile lists Sundargarh, Mayurbhanj and parts of Keonjhar.

A research map should use separate layers:

  1. Institutionally documented distribution: locations named by government or museum sources.
  2. Census or administrative data: figures with year and exact category.
  3. Village-level documentation: locations verified through records or community research.
  4. Oral-history locations: places named in recorded narratives, labelled as oral accounts.
  5. Migration destinations: places where families or groups have settled through more recent movement.

This approach avoids presenting all location data as equally certain.

5. Historical movement and migration

Migration may occur for many reasons: access to cultivable land, family networks, forest resources, employment, displacement, education or changes in local economies. Historical accounts sometimes describe movement between Chotanagpur, Singhbhum, Odisha and neighbouring areas.

Such accounts must be examined individually. A migration narrative recorded in one village cannot be used to explain the origins of every Munda settlement in Odisha.

The archive should record who told the story, where the narrator's family lived, which places were named and whether documentary evidence supports the account.

6. How to build the Munda Heritage Map

Mundari Samaj can eventually create a community heritage map. Each location record should include:

  • village or place name;
  • district and block;
  • map coordinates only with informed permission and careful consideration of privacy;
  • community name used locally;
  • language or variety reported;
  • source of information;
  • date of verification;
  • whether the information comes from an official record, field visit or oral history;
  • restrictions on public display, if any.

Do not publish precise locations of sacred, private or sensitive places without community approval.

7. A note on population figures

Population figures must always include the census year, geographical unit and category. A figure for the Scheduled Tribe entry "Munda" may not be directly comparable with a figure for people recorded under "Mundari" or another language label.

The Odisha State Tribal Museum cites Census 2011 data for its population profiles. The SCSTRTI website displays a separate Munda population figure. Before publication, editors should verify the original Census table, category definitions and any later revisions. Do not copy a number without its context.

Conclusion

Munda settlement history in Odisha is an important field for research. Existing institutional sources provide a starting map, but village histories, local documents and community interviews are necessary to build a fuller account.

Mundari Samaj should present the geography of Munda communities as a living and changing history, not as a fixed map based on one origin story.

Editorial notes

Create a verified village list only after checking source records and community consent.

Do not publish precise household locations.

Never combine Munda and Mundari counts without explaining the categories.

Include a source date and confidence label for each map layer.
