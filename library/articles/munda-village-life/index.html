The Munda Village — community life, leadership and change

2026-11-04 · 6 min read · Heritage · Mundari Samaj Editorial Desk

The village has always been the centre of Munda social, economic and cultural life — the place of the fields and the Sarna grove, of the akhra dancing ground and the founding family's burial stones. Yet no single model describes every village: settlement patterns, leadership titles, land arrangements and festival calendars differ from region to region, and every account belongs to its place and its time. One village shows what such a record can hold: Jojohatu, in Khunti subdivision, which the Census of India studied house by house for its 1961 village monograph series, published in 1963.

The people who lead

Historical accounts of Chotanagpur name the customary offices again and again. The Munda is the village headman, keeper of its secular order; the Pahan is its priest, who offers to the bongas on the village's behalf; above a single village stands the Manki, head of a group of villages (a parha or pir) — the same Manki–Munda order the British later wrote into Wilkinson's Rules for Kolhan. Villages also consult the Ojha, the diviner and healer, before festivals and in sickness. Roy's history suggests the offices began united: the founding family's heir was head in temporal and spiritual matters alike, and the Munda's and Pahan's roles separated only as villages grew. Exact duties differ by locality — a list from one district should never be pasted onto another — and today elected panchayats work alongside the customary offices.

A year of work and worship — Jojohatu, 1961

The monograph's Jojohatu is an agricultural village whose year turns with the paddy. Its festival round, recorded in detail, includes Karma — kept on the eleventh day of Bhado, and only in the years the village's advisers judge right; Sohrai in October–November, when the cattle are washed, anointed and garlanded, lamps are lit in every house and the household gods are fed; and Mage, the feast of the ancestors. The same pages preserve customs rarely written down: after a community feast the Pahan goes from house to house with a cup of handia (rice beer) as Ashirbad, a blessing — and once in twelve years the women of Jojohatu dress in men's clothes and go out on Jani Sikar, a mock hunt to the neighbouring villages. Even the differences instruct: the monograph places Jojohatu's Mage in November–December, while Roy's Ranchi account puts it at the January full moon. Villages differ, and honest records say so.

The village as memory

Official documents rarely hold a village's real archive. It lives in the founding story told by elders, the names of ancestral fields, the Sarna where the village gods dwell, the songs sung at Karma and Sohrai, and the family histories carried from house to house. That is why Mundari Samaj records village life with the narrators' consent, names places and tellers, and keeps versions side by side when they differ — variation is part of the record, not an error to be erased. If your village's story is not yet here, the Contribute page is where it can begin.
