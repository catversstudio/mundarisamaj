Mundari Dialects and Regional Differences

2026-11-04 · 4 min read · Language · Mundari Samaj Editorial Desk

Mundari is one language, spoken across a wide region of Jharkhand, Odisha and neighbouring states — and, like every widely spoken language, it changes from place to place. A word pronounced one way in a village near Khunti may sound different in Ranchi town or in Mayurbhanj, and grammar shifts a little too. Linguists who have documented Mundari over the past century usually describe this variation through four main named varieties. Knowing them helps us listen better — and helps this archive record each speaker's words faithfully, in the form in which they were actually spoken.

Four named varieties

The linguist Toshiki Osada, following the classification in Hoffmann's Encyclopaedia Mundarica, lists four dialects of Mundari, spoken mostly in Jharkhand. Hasada is the variety east of the Ranchi–Chaibasa road. Naguri is spoken west of the same road. Tamaria — also called Latar — belongs to the Panchpargana area: Tamar, Bundu, Rahe, Sonahatu and Silli. Kera is the variety of Ranchi city and its surroundings, spoken largely by Oraon (Kurukh) families who have made Mundari their own. The Central Institute of Indian Languages' Mundari corpus documentation names the same four — Hasada, Naguri, Tamaria and Kera — as the primary varieties of the language.

How the varieties differ

The differences are real but modest beside what the varieties share. Osada notes a difference in sounds: Naguri and Kera possess aspirated stops — sounds of the kh, th type — that Hasada and Tamaria do not have. The CIIL corpus description observes that Kera's core vocabulary is close to that of the other three varieties, but its verb formations are noticeably different, both in how new verb forms are derived and in how they are inflected. Osada's own widely used grammatical description of Mundari is based mainly on Hasada — which is one reason Hasada appears so often in books. That is a fact about where documentation happened, not a ranking of the varieties themselves.

Where scholars draw the lines differently

The boundaries of a language are not always drawn the same way. Bhumij, for example, is listed in many sources as a separate language; the linguist Gregory Anderson has suggested it may instead be a variety of the Tamaria (Latar) group. It is spoken across parts of Jharkhand and in Mayurbhanj district of Odisha. Reference works do not all place Bhumij in the same position, and classifications of Mundari's varieties can differ in detail from one scholar to another. The four-variety picture above is the most commonly cited one — but it should be read as a map drawn by linguists, not as walls between villages.

No variety is the 'wrong' Mundari

It matters to say this plainly: there is no 'incorrect' Mundari. A variety is simply the speech a community has shaped in its own place over generations — in the Panchpargana villages, in Ranchi's Oraon neighbourhoods, in the Hasada country east of the Ranchi–Chaibasa road, and in the Mundari-speaking districts of northern Odisha such as Mayurbhanj, Keonjhar and Sundargarh, where the language lives alongside Odia. Differences in pronunciation or vocabulary between these places are the normal life of a language, not errors to be corrected into somebody else's speech.

Why this archive records your variety

This is why Mundari Samaj asks contributors not only for a word, song or story, but also for where it comes from. When a word is recorded, we note the village and the variety it belongs to, and we keep the contributor's own form rather than silently rewriting it into a different variety. A dictionary or story collection that mixes varieties without labels teaches confusion; one that labels them honestly teaches the language as it truly is — one language, spoken with many local voices, all of them worth preserving.
