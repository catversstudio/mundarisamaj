Khuntkatti — the land of the first clearers

2026-11-04 · 6 min read · History · Mundari Samaj Editorial Desk

Khuntkatti is the customary land institution at the heart of old Munda village life in Chotanagpur. It rests on a simple founding fact: a family — or a line of related families — first cleared the forest, laid out a village and made fields. Those first clearers and their descendants, the khuntkattidars, held the village land together, not as a market commodity but as the inheritance of the founding line. The institution joins land, kinship and memory in a single word.

Roy's account of the founding

In The Mundas and Their Country (1912), S. C. Roy describes how each founding family made its clearances in the virgin forest; the clearing became the village (hatu), later known as the khuntkatti-hatu — the village of the original settlers' family. The founder laid down the boundaries by lighting bonfires at the four corners and joining them with straight lines; the boundary-gods (Siman-bongako) were believed to keep watch over them, and Mundas regarded the lines as sacred and inviolable. Within those bounds, Roy writes, everything — cultivable land and waste, hills, jungles and streams — became the common property of the village-family. Groves were set apart for the village gods (hatu bongako): the Sarnas. Every member of the khuntkatti group could take wood from the village jungles for house and field; later settlers who joined the village (parja horoko) in some places needed the khuntkattidars' leave.

Headmen of the founding line

When the founder died, Roy records, his eldest son became the patriarchal head of the branches of the family. In the earliest villages the two great offices had not yet separated: the same head carried the duties later divided between the Munda, the secular head of the village, and the Pahan, its priest. As families multiplied, the branches spread into the clans (kili) of the village, but the founding line's authority over the land remained the reference point of the whole order.

What the law says

Colonial surveys and settlement operations tried to force this custom into record-of-rights categories, and the Chota Nagpur Tenancy Act, 1908, gave it statutory form. Section 7 recognises the "raiyat having khuntkatti rights" — a holder of land reclaimed from jungle by the original founders of the village or their male-line descendants; Section 8 defines the "Mundari khuntkattidar". A whole chapter of the Act (Chapter XVIII, sections 239–256) lays down special provisions for Mundari khuntkattidars, and Section 240 bars the transfer of such a tenure in execution of a court decree, with only narrow exceptions — a shield against exactly the land-grabbing that had caused the nineteenth century's rebellions. Scholars note the double edge: the Act safeguarded the system, but in law it recast the khuntkattidar as a special kind of tenant, where custom had treated the founding line as the land's owner.

Why it still matters

Khuntkatti explains why land, for Munda families, is never only an economic asset: it is ancestors, identity and belonging made visible. The erosion of khuntkatti by landlords, moneylenders and revenue farmers fed grievance after grievance — the Kol Rebellion, the Sardari Larai and the Ulgulan. One caution belongs with the word: this is the Chotanagpur institution, recorded there. Munda settlements in Odisha and elsewhere have their own settlement histories and terms, which deserve to be documented on their own evidence, not assumed to match.
