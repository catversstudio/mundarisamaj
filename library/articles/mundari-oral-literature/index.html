Mundari Oral Literature and Proverbs

2026-11-04 · 4 min read · Language · Mundari Samaj Editorial Desk

Before Mundari had printed books, it had something older and, for centuries, sufficient: a literature carried in memory and voice. Tales told after dark, songs for work and festival, riddles traded as a game of wit, proverbs that compressed judgement into a single line — together these formed the Munda community's library. Nothing was written down, yet very little was left unrecorded; it was recorded in people. This article looks at how it lived, what it carried, and how parts of it were written down.

The evening school: folklore in the giti-ora

S. C. Roy, whose The Mundas and Their Country (1912) remains a foundational record, put it directly: for the ordinary Munda of his time, the only vehicle of instruction and culture was folklore — kahani — consisting of narratives or folktales, riddles and proverbs. These were 'recited and learnt in the evening' by the young bachelors and maidens in their respective giti-oras, the village youth dormitories, after the day's work was over. The dormitory was at once a sleeping-house, a club and, in Roy's phrase, an educational seminary. Roy closed his book with the literature itself: his contents give folk-tales, proverbs, and a final gathering of songs running to more than thirty pages.

Tales that carry memory and teaching

The commonest class of tales, Roy records, were prose stories called kaji-kahani; he prints one, 'The Tiger and the Thief', as a sample. Other tales carry far older freight. The Asur legend, which Roy retells, remembers the Asurs, an iron-smelting people, and tells how their furnaces, worked day and night, sent up a heat that dried the streams and scorched the earth, until the creatures of the world complained to Sing Bonga, who sent bird messengers ordering the Asurs to work by day or by night, but not both. The Asurs refused, and the tale moves toward their fall. A story like this is many things at once: a memory of an earlier people and their craft, an explanation of the world, and a teaching about excess and its price. Oral tales also carried the community's law in portable form — Roy records the solemn oath formula of the Mundas, invoking 'Sing-bonga on high, and the Panch on earth', a whole theory of authority held in one remembered line.

Songs and riddles

Songs accompanied the year's round of festivals, dances and work, and Roy's collection shows they could also carry commentary on life as it was being lived — including songs of indignation at wrongs suffered. Riddles were the evening's lighter discipline: posed and solved in the giti-ora, they sharpened the young in the precise, playful use of their language — its sounds, double meanings and images drawn from fields, forests and animals. Between tale, song, riddle and proverb, a young person absorbed vocabulary, history, custom and conduct without ever opening a book.

Written down twice: 1912 and 1961

Twice in the twentieth century, recorders wrote this oral world down at length. Roy's 1912 book printed Mundari tales, riddles, proverbs and songs alongside his history of the community. Half a century later, the Census of India's village monograph on Jojohatu (1961, published 1963), by S. D. Prasad, preserved another great tale in full: the legend of Karma and Dharma, which explains the Karma festival. It tells of Karma, who in anger destroyed the Karam branches and his brother's seedlings, lost his wealth, and journeyed in search of Karam Raja, by whose grace fortune returned. The monograph sets the tale beside the festival as Jojohatu kept it — on the eleventh day of Bhado, with branches of the Bhelwa tree, song and all-night dancing, no sacrifice, and the festival observed in alternate years. Both tales are now retold on this website — 'The Asur Legend' and 'Karma and Dharma — the legend of Karam Raja' — each with its source named.

Proverbs: a shelf still being filled

A word of honesty about proverbs. Roy printed a few pages of Mundari proverbial lore as samples, remarking on how much judgement the form can carry. But a proverb is not only a sentence; it belongs to a place, a speaker and an occasion, and its force often lies in when it is said. A trustworthy collection must therefore record each proverb with its source — who used it, where, and in what variety of Mundari — rather than gathering unattributed sayings from anywhere. Such a sourced Mundari proverb collection is still being built for this archive, and we would rather leave the shelf partly empty than fill it with lines we cannot vouch for. If your family keeps proverbs, songs, riddles or tales, the Contribute page explains how to share them with place and teller recorded — so the next recorder of this literature can be the community itself.
