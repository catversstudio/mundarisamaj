Reading Census and Official Records Responsibly

2026-11-04 · 3 min read · Heritage · Mundari Samaj Editorial Desk

Introduction

Official records are essential for researching Munda history in Odisha. Census tables, government profiles, district gazetteers and tribal research publications can help identify communities and understand changes over time. However, these sources use specific definitions and categories. Reading them without context can produce misleading claims.

This guide explains how Mundari Samaj should use official data.

1. A category is not always the same as a language

The Census of India and government tribal profiles may list a Scheduled Tribe community, a subgroup, a language or a related administrative label. These categories serve different purposes.

For example, the Census of India catalogue for Odisha's 2011 ST-14 table lists "Munda" and "Mundari" separately among its tribe labels. The Odisha State Tribal Museum also maintains separate Munda and Mundari profiles.

This does not mean the labels can be added together automatically. Editors must inspect the original table and its instructions before interpreting the data.

2. Always record the year and geographical unit

A population number is incomplete unless the reader knows:

  • the census or survey year;
  • the state, district, block or village covered;
  • the exact category counted;
  • whether the number refers to total population, a language response or another measure;
  • the source table and page.

A 1971 police-station-wise distribution table is not a current village list. A 2011 district census figure should not be presented as a 2026 population estimate.

3. Understand the source behind a table

The SCSTRTI publication Demographic Profile of Scheduled Tribes in Odisha lists Munda and Mundari in several Sundargarh tribal sub-plan blocks. Its source note refers to the SCSTRTI Universal Bench Mark Survey conducted in 1978–79 and Census of India 1971 police-station-wise distribution.

This historical data is useful for studying the administrative record of the period. It should not be described as a current survey.

4. Separate official labels from self-identification

Administrative terminology may differ from the names people use for themselves. Historical records may also contain spellings or labels that are no longer preferred.

When possible, record:

  • the exact term used by the source;
  • the local name used by the community;
  • the date and purpose of the record;
  • any known difference between the two.

Do not silently replace an older official label in a quotation. Explain it in context and avoid repeating derogatory or outdated terminology unnecessarily.

5. A practical source-checking form

Before publishing a statistic or district claim, editors should complete this checklist:

  • What is the original source?
  • What year does it describe?
  • Which geographical unit is counted?
  • What exactly is the category?
  • Is it a community count, language count or survey estimate?
  • Does the source define the category?
  • Can the original table be accessed?
  • Is a newer comparable source available?
  • Has a reviewer checked the interpretation?

If these questions cannot be answered, omit the number or clearly label the information as unverified.

Conclusion

Official data can strengthen Munda history, but only when its definitions and limitations are made clear. Mundari Samaj should publish numbers with their original context and should never combine unlike categories to create a larger or more impressive figure.

Editorial notes

Never add Munda and Mundari totals without documentary justification.

Do not use third-party summaries as substitutes for original census tables.

Preserve the source note and survey year whenever quoting historical administrative data.
