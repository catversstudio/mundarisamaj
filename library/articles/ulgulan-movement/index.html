The Ulgulan — Causes, Events and Consequences (1899–1900)

2026-11-04 · 4 min read · History · Mundari Samaj Editorial Desk

Ulgulan — 'the Great Tumult' — is the name the Munda people give to the rising of 1899–1900. It was far more than the story of one leader. Behind it lay forty years of petitions and lawsuits over land; within it, a religious renewal that swept the Munda country; and after it, changes in law and administration that are still felt in Jharkhand today.

The land question

The Mundas' customary land system, khuntkatti, vested rights in the descendants of the families who had first cleared the forest and founded each village. Through the nineteenth century this order was broken down. Landlords brought in non-tribal farmers and contractors — thikadars — who took over village lands, demanded ever higher rents and exacted unpaid forced labour (beth-begari). These outsiders were known as dikus. By 1874 the authority of the old Munda and Oraon chiefs had been almost entirely displaced, and in some villages the original proprietors had been reduced to labourers on their own ancestral land.

The Sardari Larai: forty years of petitions

From 1858 the Munda and Oraon sardars — hereditary chiefs and headmen — waged the Sardari Larai, a struggle conducted largely within the law. Their case rested on a simple claim: the Adivasis were the first people to clear the lands of Chotanagpur, and so held an inalienable right to them. Between 1858 and 1895 they petitioned the highest levels of the colonial state, up to the Viceroy of India and even the Secretary of State in London. The petitions won almost nothing. From about 1890 the sardars turned against European missionaries and officials as well, and a September 1892 plot against contractors and missionaries came to nothing for want of organisation.

A prophet's call

That leader was Birsa Munda. In 1895 he proclaimed a divine calling, urged his people to worship one God and give up the worship of bongas (spirits), and declared the reign of Queen Victoria over: 'Abua raj ete jana, maharani raj tundu jana' — let the kingdom of the Queen be ended, and our kingdom be established. He ordered the raiyats to pay no rent. Thousands — Mundas, Oraons and Kharias — accepted his teaching; he was arrested in August 1895 and imprisoned for two years. After his release he rebuilt his following in secret, while the terrible famines of 1896–97 and 1899–1900 deepened the anger his message spoke to.

The rising

Around Christmas 1899 some 7,000 men and women gathered to herald the Ulgulan, which spread across Khunti, Tamar, Basia and Ranchi. On Christmas Eve, attacks were made on churches in the Ranchi and Singhbhum country; the missions at Murhu and Sarwada were among the main targets, though the Birsaites declared their real enemy was the British, not Christian Mundas. On 5 January 1900 two police constables were killed at Etkedih. On 7 January the rebels attacked the Khunti police station, killed a constable and burnt the houses of local shopkeepers. The government set a reward of Rs 500 on Birsa's head, and Commissioner A. Fobes, with Deputy Commissioner H. C. Streattfield, reached Khunti with a force of 150 men.

Dombari Buru

The rebels concentrated on Sail Rakab hill at Dombari Buru, near Khunti. There, on 9 January 1900, government troops fired on Birsa's followers. How many died has never been settled: official records acknowledged only small numbers, while in Munda memory the firing was a massacre in which hundreds — men, women and children — were killed, and the hill is remembered with grief. Birsa himself escaped into the Singhbhum hills.

Capture, trial and death

On 3 February 1900 Birsa was captured in the Jamkopai forest, in the Porahat country near Chakradharpur. The crackdown was sweeping: by the Deputy Commissioner's own figures, 460 tribals were made accused in fifteen criminal cases; 63 were convicted, one sentenced to death and 39 transported for life. Six prisoners, Birsa among them, died in custody during the trials. Birsa Munda died in Ranchi jail on 9 June 1900, aged twenty-four, and with him the rising ended.

What changed

The Ulgulan failed in its immediate aim — no Munda Raj was established — but it forced the colonial state to confront the land question it had ignored for forty years. In 1905 the government created the Khunti subdivision, bringing administration closer to the heartland of the revolt. In 1908 it enacted the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act, which recognised khuntkatti rights in law and prohibited the transfer of tribal land to non-tribals — a protection that remains on the statute book. In memory, Birsa lives on as Dharti Aba, the Father of the Earth, and the self-rule he proclaimed in 1899 became one of the founding inspirations of the movement that, a century later, won the state of Jharkhand.
