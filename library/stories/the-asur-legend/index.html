The Asur Legend — how Sing Bonga outwitted the iron-smelters

Traditional tale · recorded by S. C. Roy (1912) · Munda oral tradition

[{'type': 'p', 'text': 'Long ago, the Asurs — a people whose craft was smelting iron — worked their furnaces day and night without rest. The heat dried up the streams, tanks and pools, and scorched all the vegetation of the earth, until the beasts came and complained to Sing Bonga, the Sun-God, the supreme deity of the Mundas.'}, {'type': 'h2', 'text': 'The messengers'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': 'Sing Bonga first thought of slaying the Asurs, but his consort counselled cunning instead. He sent the Dingchua and the Kerketa birds with an order: the furnaces were to be worked only by day, or only by night. The Asurs laughed at the messengers and smeared them with coal-dust and iron-ore dust — which is why those birds are black and grey to this day. Golden and silver vultures sent after them were beaten with hammer and iron pincers, and the Lipo and the Kuhu were driven away the same way.'}, {'type': 'h2', 'text': 'The boy with the itching sores'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': "Then Sing Bonga came down to earth in disguise — as the Toro Kora, a boy covered in itching sores — and took shelter in the house of the old couple Lutkum Haram and Lutkum Buria, guarding their drying grain from the fowls. Playing guli and kati — marbles and pickets — against twelve Asur boys and thirteen Deota boys, the boy's gulis of eggs and katis of rice-cakes smashed the iron ones of his opponents to pieces."}, {'type': 'p', 'text': "In those days the Asurs' furnaces began to fail. Divination with the sup, the winnowing fan, pointed to the Toro Kora, and reading the rice-grains he prescribed sacrifices in turn — a white fowl, then a white goat, then a young sheep. Each restored the furnaces only for a little while. At last he declared that a human sacrifice was required. The Asurs tried to buy a Munda child, but the Munda parents scorned the offer — so the Toro Kora offered himself. Before going, he consoled the old couple with a promise: he would establish the Pahan's son under a tree in the middle of the village, to offer sacrifices for them at the Baha flower feast, at Batauli, and at the Mage feast."}, {'type': 'h2', 'text': 'The treasure in the furnace'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': 'Two fasting virgins worked new bellows for three days and nights. And when the furnace was opened, the boy stepped out radiant as the morning sun, covered in gold and silver, and carrying a precious plate and bowl. He told the greedy Asurs that far more treasure lay inside, hidden under the wings of a golden and a silver vulture — and that every man must go in together, so that none could rob another. When all the Asur men were inside, Sing Bonga ordered the women to plaster up the furnace, kindle the fire and work the bellows. Sounds came from within; the women were told the men were quarrelling over the spoils, and that the red liquid they saw was betel juice being spat out. When the furnace was opened at last, there was nothing inside but charred bones and ashes.'}, {'type': 'h2', 'text': 'How the bongas came to be'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': 'Then Sing Bonga revealed himself. The Asur women he hurled to the mountains and rocks, the deep waters and uplands, the woods, springs, meadows and groves — there their spirits would dwell forever as bongas, the presiding deities of those places, to be consulted by the soothsayers through rice-grains and lamplight, and to receive offerings from the Pahan. So the legend explains, all at once, the bongas of hill and stream, the village priesthood of the Pahan, and the calling of the soothsayers.'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': 'Retold, without addition, from the text recorded by Sarat Chandra Roy in “The Mundas and Their Country” (1912), Appendix I — who calls this legend “first and foremost in point of sanctity and popularity” among the traditions of the Mundas. The same legend is also preserved in the Encyclopaedia Mundarica.'}]
