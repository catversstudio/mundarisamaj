Karma and Dharma — the legend of Karam Raja

Traditional tale · recorded at Jojohatu (Census of India, 1961) · Jojohatu village, Khunti

[{'type': 'p', 'text': "At Jojohatu, a Munda village in the Khunti hills, the Karma festival is kept for wealth, prosperity and pleasure in life — and this is the legend its people tell of how the festival began. It was recorded there by the Census of India in 1961. Like all such tales it exists in other versions in other villages; this is Jojohatu's own."}, {'type': 'h2', 'text': 'How dancing came to earth'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': 'In the early days, dancing was not known in this world. It lived only in the palace of King Indra in the heavens, where the parees — the nymphs — danced. In the palace there lived a bhaunra, a hornet, and his wife. One day the hornet found a beautiful flower and gave it to his wife, warning her never to show it to the nymphs. But she was so enamoured of the flower that she came down daily to fetch more from the garden of King Aja on earth — and one day, she showed it. The nymphs were desperate to have such flowers, and that evening they refused to dance.'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': "So Indra, the nymphs and the hornet rode down in Indra's chariot to King Aja's garden and plucked the flowers — and it became their daily practice. At last the gardener detected the theft and told the king, who had a special ash, treated with magic, sprayed upon the flowers. That night the chariot and the nymphs were caught fast, motionless, unable to return to heaven. Indra explained himself: the flowers decorated the nymphs when they performed the Karma dance, the source of all the wealth and prosperity of his kingdom, and he advised King Aja to perform the dance too. But the chariot could move again, Aja said, only if touched by a virgin girl who had fasted that day. Such a girl was found — a widow's daughter, who that very morning had quarrelled with her mother, so that neither had cooked or eaten. She touched the chariot, Indra returned to heaven, and from that day King Aja performed the Karma dance and grew from wealth to greater wealth. The art of dancing had come to earth."}, {'type': 'h2', 'text': "Karma's journey for salt"}, {'type': 'p', 'text': "King Aja had two sons, Anayashri and Danyashri, who were the fathers of Karma and Dharma. In those days salt was scarce in the kingdom, and on Indra's advice Karma was sent to Katak to bring salt. Merchant after merchant refused him, until at last one big businessman sold him a large stock, and Karma came home with it loaded on bullock carts. Nearing the village, he sent a messenger ahead to Dharma, asking to be received properly after his long journey. But Dharma was engaged in the worship of the Karma god, and sent back word: wait. A second messenger brought the same answer."}, {'type': 'p', 'text': "Enraged, Karma went himself to his brother's place, leaving the carts behind — and in his fury he cut down all the Karam branches, destroyed the articles of worship, and quarrelled with Dharma. That night the brothers parted. Karma went back for his salt and found the bullock carts all empty. At home his building stood in ruins, and his wife and children were in rags, and hungry."}, {'type': 'h2', 'text': 'The empty carts'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': "Karma went to Dharma's house, where delicious dishes were being served to the guests, as is usual after the Karma festival. He was asked to wait — guests must have precedence over family. He went again, and was met with the same reply. In despair he took his wife and children to work in Dharma's fields; and when the day's work was done and he asked for his wages, Dharma gave wages first to the outside labourers, and only after them to Karma, who was but a member of the family. Enraged a second time, Karma went back to the field and began uprooting all the seedlings he had transplanted. But as fast as he tore them out, a beautiful man was transplanting them again. The man told him to worship Karam Raja — for all his troubles had come from his own bad Karam, his luck."}, {'type': 'h2', 'text': 'In search of Karam Raja'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': 'So Karma set out in search of Karam Raja. On the way he met a tortoise hanging from the branch of a tree in great pain, and it begged him to pray before Karam Raja for its salvation. Further on he met a woman with a borsi — a small earthen pot used as a hearth — fixed upon her head, and she made the same request. Then he came to a tank full of insects, where a crocodile lay with a big palm tree growing above its body, and it too asked Karma to pray for it.'}, {'type': 'p', 'text': "At last Karma saw Karam Raja floating in a river. He jumped into the water to touch the deity's feet, but because of his sin he could not reach them. For hours he tried, until, dejected and exhausted, he was on the point of sinking. Then Karam Raja took pity on him and saved him. Karma prayed, and all his troubles were over: his lost wealth and prosperity returned to him. And he did not forget the others — he prayed for the tortoise, the woman and the crocodile, and they too were relieved of their sufferings. From that very day, the legend says, people began to have great faith in the Karma festival, and to perform it with zeal and enthusiasm."}, {'type': 'p', 'text': "In Jojohatu's own account, the festival is observed on the eleventh day of Bhado (August–September), in a household where the Ojha has advised it — sometimes after a shoot, an “ankoor”, appears in the paddy. Virgin girls bring baskets of earth from the field and the Karam god is installed in clay in the courtyard; bachelors cut two Bhelwa branches in the forest, and the branches are planted and worshipped with flowers. No sacrifice of any kind is permitted; handia, rice beer, is freely used, and the dancing goes on all night and from door to door the next day. The festival is performed every alternate year, for it is believed that in the year between, the Karam god goes to visit his other relatives."}, {'type': 'p', 'text': 'Retold, without addition, from the legend recorded in “Jojohatu, a Munda Village” — Census of India 1961, Village Survey Monograph (Khunti Subdivision, District Ranchi), published 1963.'}]
